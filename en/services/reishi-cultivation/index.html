Reishi Cultivation (Ganoderma lucidum)

In-house cultivation of Reishi from mycelium to fruiting bodies, with antler morphology control, natural substrates and full traceability.

At GoMushroom, we cultivate Reishi entirely in-house — from mycelium propagation, substrate preparation and sterilization, to controlled fruiting body development. We operate in small batches, where each grow bag is manually inspected, ensuring stable growth conditions, consistent raw material quality, and full traceability from selected strain to extract or tincture.

We deliberately favor antler-type morphology, as research suggests this growth form may be associated with a higher concentration of key bioactive compounds — particularly triterpenes (ganoderic acids).

This approach allows for tighter control over the quality of raw material used in medicinal mushroom extraction, while also improving batch-to-batch consistency. More about our quality approach can be found on the Heavy Metals Testing page.

It all starts with mycelium

The mushroom we see is only the fruiting body — the organism itself is the mycelium, a network of fine threads growing through its food. On agar it spreads into a flat circular colony; in a substrate bag it grows through the entire block and binds it into one solid piece.

A Reishi tincture therefore does not begin with a mushroom, but with the mycelium that will go on to produce one. From here on, everything depends on two things: how good the culture is, and how cleanly we work with it.

From mushroom to agar plate

A culture begins with a mushroom. We take a piece of tissue from a selected fruiting body, working in sterile airflow — at that moment the tissue is completely unprotected, and any mould spore in the room would outrun it.

The piece goes onto sterilized agar, and the plate goes into the dark at a suitable temperature. From that fragment, the mycelium slowly grows outward into a circular colony.

Selecting and propagating the culture

Once the mycelium has covered most of the agar, we compare the plates against each other. They do not grow alike: some colonies are denser, more even and faster than others.

We pick the best plate and propagate from it — cutting pieces of colonized medium out of the colony and transferring them onto several fresh plates. One good culture thus becomes a working set of plates, all from the same source.

Grain spawn

Mycelium cannot go straight from an agar plate into substrate — there is simply too little of it. Grain spawn is the step in between, where the mycelium multiplies to a usable volume.

We sterilize the grain in the autoclave — a 30-litre vessel that works under pressure and so reaches temperatures above the boiling point of water. Once cooled, it is inoculated with mycelium from the plate that came out best in selection. The mycelium grows through the grain, turning every kernel into inoculum for the next step.

Substrate and colonization

We prepare the substrate ourselves. Beech sawdust forms the base, with wheat bran, millet, and rye added to it. The sawdust provides structure and lignin, while the grain and bran supply the nitrogen the mycelium needs to grow. Our rye and wheat bran come from Kmetija Malerič, a Slovenian farm that grows both organically.

The mixture is hydrated, weighed into filter bags, and loaded into the atmospheric sterilizer. It works at normal air pressure and reaches around 95 °C, less than the autoclave — so we extend the exposure instead. Held long enough at that temperature, the treatment achieves an effect comparable to sterilization. Without it nothing that follows works: in an untreated substrate, moulds outrun the mycelium before it properly gets going.

Once cooled, the bags are inoculated with grain spawn and moved into the dark at a stable temperature. The mycelium first grows in separate strands, then knits into a single white layer that binds the whole block together. Bags that colonize unevenly or show signs of contamination are discarded.

Antler morphology as a controlled response

Antler morphology represents a natural physiological response to specific cultivation conditions. Under limited ventilation (elevated CO₂) and controlled light exposure, Reishi develops a branched antler-like structure instead of a cap.

Controlled studies have shown that adjusting ventilation and light parameters enables reliable formation of antler-type fruiting bodies, while also influencing the profile of bioactive compounds and antioxidant potential of extracts.

Numerous triterpenes and other bioactive compounds have been identified in antler-type G. lucidum, supporting the concept that this morphology may contribute to a richer secondary metabolite profile.

Why in-house cultivation

  • Greater control over raw material quality
  • More consistent input for downstream extraction
  • Full traceability from mycelium to final product
  • Ability to tailor morphology and cultivation parameters to target compound profiles

In-house cultivation is therefore an integral part of our laboratory approach, connecting cultivation, extraction, and formulation into a single, controlled system.
