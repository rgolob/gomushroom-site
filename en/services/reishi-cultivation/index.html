Reishi Cultivation (Ganoderma lucidum)

In-house cultivation of Reishi from mycelium to fruiting bodies, with antler morphology control, natural substrates and full traceability.

At GoMushroom, we cultivate Reishi entirely in-house — from mycelium propagation, substrate preparation and sterilization, to controlled fruiting body development. We operate in small batches, where each grow bag is manually inspected, ensuring stable growth conditions, consistent raw material quality, and full traceability from selected strain to extract or tincture.

We deliberately favor antler-type morphology, as research suggests this growth form may be associated with a higher concentration of key bioactive compounds — particularly triterpenes (ganoderic acids).

This approach allows for tighter control over the quality of raw material used in medicinal mushroom extraction, while also improving batch-to-batch consistency. More about our quality approach can be found on the Heavy Metals Testing page.

From strain to fruiting body

  • Cloning and maintaining the strain on agar plates
  • Propagating the mycelium through grain spawn
  • Substrate preparation: beech sawdust, wheat bran, millet, and rye
  • Sterilization of the substrate in an autoclave
  • Inoculation and full colonization of the block
  • Controlled fruiting conditions: temperature, humidity, CO₂, and light

We carry out every one of these steps ourselves, in small batches with manual inspection of each grow unit. That is also why every batch of tincture can be traced back to the strain it grew from.

Strain and cloning

Every batch begins on an agar plate. We transfer a piece of tissue from a selected fruiting body onto the medium, where the mycelium grows into its characteristic circular colony. Once the plate is fully colonized, we cut out pieces of the medium and carry them forward — onto a fresh plate, or into grain spawn.

This keeps the strain ours. Rather than buying in mycelium for every batch, we maintain our own culture and know which fruiting body it came from.

Spawn and inoculation

From the agar plate, the mycelium moves onto grain. Colonized grain spawn is the intermediate step: it multiplies the mycelium to the volume needed to inoculate substrate bags.

Everything that gets opened — plates, spawn, and bags — is opened in filtered air beside a lit burner. This is the most sensitive part of the process, as a single contamination means a lost bag.

Substrate and sterilization

We prepare the substrate ourselves. Beech sawdust forms the base, with wheat bran, millet, and rye added to it. The sawdust provides structure and lignin, while the grain and bran supply the nitrogen the mycelium needs to grow.

The mixture is hydrated, weighed into filter bags, and loaded into the autoclave. Sterilization is the precondition for everything that follows — in a non-sterile substrate, moulds outrun the mycelium before it properly gets going.

Colonization

Once cooled, the bags are inoculated with grain spawn and moved into the dark at a stable temperature. The mycelium first grows in separate strands, then knits into a single white layer that binds the whole substrate block together.

Only a fully colonized bag goes on to fruiting. Bags that colonize unevenly or show signs of contamination are discarded.

Antler morphology as a controlled response

Antler morphology represents a natural physiological response to specific cultivation conditions. Under limited ventilation (elevated CO₂) and controlled light exposure, Reishi develops a branched antler-like structure instead of a cap.

Controlled studies have shown that adjusting ventilation and light parameters enables reliable formation of antler-type fruiting bodies, while also influencing the profile of bioactive compounds and antioxidant potential of extracts.

Numerous triterpenes and other bioactive compounds have been identified in antler-type G. lucidum, supporting the concept that this morphology may contribute to a richer secondary metabolite profile.

Why in-house cultivation

  • Greater control over raw material quality
  • More consistent input for downstream extraction
  • Full traceability from mycelium to final product
  • Ability to tailor morphology and cultivation parameters to target compound profiles

In-house cultivation is therefore an integral part of our laboratory approach, connecting cultivation, extraction, and formulation into a single, controlled system.
